02 — Case Study · Rhythm Healthcare

Bringing Design Consistency to a Cardiac Monitoring Platform

Redesigning a clinician's daily workflow on a remote patient monitoring platform processing 1.2M+ transmissions a year — and hearing "you made my work easier."

RoleSole Designer
ClientRhythm Healthcare
IndustryHealthcare · Cardiac Monitoring
Timeline2023–2024
Hero — redesigned patient overview with persistent clinical data panel
Overview

The short version

Rhythm Healthcare is a cardiac monitoring and remote patient monitoring platform — processing over 1.2 million transmissions per year across 100K+ patients. When I joined as the sole designer, I found a product built by a rotating mix of freelancers, agencies, and in-house designers over time.

The result: visual inconsistencies across screens, two conflicting design systems (Polaris for design, Chakra for development), and clinicians losing time on basic tasks. Over one year, I audited the product, interviewed clinicians twice, redesigned the patient overview, restructured the alert system, and proposed a unified design direction — work that directly contributed to winning new clients.

Background

The setup

Rhythm Healthcare brought me on as the sole designer for their cardiac monitoring platform — already live, already serving clinicians daily, already carrying the scars of multiple designers' conflicting work. My job: make it consistent, make it usable, make clinicians trust it.

I worked across CRM (Cardiac Rhythm Monitoring) and RPM (Remote Patient Monitoring) simultaneously, handling 3–4 workstreams in parallel as a solo designer — constant context-switching between feature work, usability improvements, system-level proposals, and clinician testing.

Integrations & scale

  • Device integrations with Abbott, Medtronic, Boston Scientific, and Biotronik
  • EHR sync with Epic, Athena, Cerner, and NextGen
  • 1.2M+ transmissions per year across 100K+ patients
The Problem

Three problems stacked on top of each other

01

The product was patched together by multiple designers

Different freelancers, agencies, and in-house designers had contributed over time. The frontend code reflected this — patches layered on patches. The same component looked different across screens. When I spoke with the frontend team, they confirmed it: inconsistencies in code came directly from inconsistencies in the designs they'd been given.

02

Two design systems were fighting each other

During my audit, I found the core issue — previous designers had used Polaris while developers built with Chakra UI. Two incompatible systems running in parallel, causing every handoff to introduce visual drift.

03

Nobody had talked to the clinicians

The product worked, but nobody had sat down with the nurses using it daily to understand what was actually slowing them down.

Audit — same component appearing differently across screens
Execution

What I did

1. Audited the product end-to-end

Before designing anything, I audited every screen — cataloging inconsistencies, broken patterns, and gaps. The most significant finding: the same component looked visually different across screens. Buttons, cards, data tables — all had subtle variations depending on which designer had built that screen. The team knew the product felt inconsistent, but they hadn't mapped the full extent of it. This audit became the foundation for everything that followed.

2. Interviewed clinicians — discovery

I spoke with 3–4 nurses and clinicians about their daily workflow. Focused, practical conversations — not a multi-month research study. What I learned:

  • They scan patients by priority, not alphabetically
  • They rarely need insurance info and provider details at the same time as clinical data
  • The most common action — checking a patient's latest reading — took too many clicks
  • Switching between different data views was where errors happened most
  • Alert noise was constant — critical alerts got buried alongside routine transmissions

3. Redesigned the patient overview with a 3-tab structure

Instead of one overwhelming screen that showed everything at once — personal info, providers, insurance, clinical data — I separated it:

  • Tab 1: Personal Info — demographics, contact details, identification
  • Tab 2: Providers — care team, referring physicians, specialist contacts
  • Tab 3: Insurance — coverage, authorization, billing details

The clinical data (readings, alerts, rhythm strips) stayed in a persistent panel — always visible regardless of which tab is active. The most critical information is never hidden behind a tab.

The design decision: information that changes per-interaction (which tab you need) is separated from information that's always relevant (clinical status). This mirrors how clinicians actually think — "who is this patient?" is a different question from "what does their heart data say?"

Redesigned patient overview — 3-tab structure with persistent clinical data panel

4. Restructured the Activity panel with priority-based ordering

The Activity panel on the right side of the patient view existed, but it was poorly organized. I redesigned it with a priority-based structure — Red Alerts surface to the top, followed by Yellow Alerts, then routine transmissions, calls, and notes. I introduced a color-coded severity system (red/yellow/green) so clinicians can glance at the panel and instantly know if something needs attention. Color and position communicate urgency — no reading required.

Activity panel — priority-based ordering with color-coded severity

5. Built hi-fi prototypes and validated with clinicians

I skipped wireframes. This was intentional, not a shortcut. The end users were internal clinicians — they can't evaluate grey boxes and placeholder text. Showing them wireframes would get feedback on "this looks unfinished," not on whether the workflow works.

Instead, I built hi-fi screens using a mix of existing and new components and created clickable prototypes that clinicians could actually experience. One round of feedback was enough to reach a design they were confident in.

"You made my work easier."
— Clinician feedback

The designs were strong enough that leadership used them in client presentations — and those presentations directly contributed to acquiring new clients.

Challenge

A challenge I faced

The Polaris/Chakra fragmentation was the hardest problem. It wasn't just a visual issue — it was structural. Every design I handed off got subtly changed in translation because the dev team's component library didn't match mine. Fixing individual screens was treating symptoms. The disease was two incompatible systems.

I mapped the full scope of the problem — documenting exactly where and how the fragmentation manifested across the product. I researched approaches to unifying the system, explored Tailwind CSS as a migration direction, and presented my findings to leadership. The approach was well-received. But organizational changes meant the migration never started during my time. The fix I'd identified outlasted my tenure.

What I took from this: identifying a systemic problem and building the case for fixing it is design work — even when the fix doesn't ship on your timeline. And the research itself significantly deepened my understanding of scalable design systems — how token architectures work across design and code, and what it takes to migrate a live product from one system to another without breaking things.

Credits

Honest credit

I was the sole designer, which meant full ownership of every design decision. But it also meant no design peer to challenge my thinking. The clinician interviews helped fill that gap — real user feedback was my check against designing in a vacuum.

I also want to be honest about scope: I proposed a role-based contextual navigation system (different nav items for different user roles), but it was in design when I left — it didn't ship. The patient overview, Activity panel, and the screens used in client presentations — those shipped and are in use.

Outcome

What shipped

  • Redesigned patient overview with 3-tab structure and persistent clinical data panel
  • Priority-based Activity panel with color-coded severity system
  • Hi-fi prototypes validated with clinicians across two rounds of engagement
  • Screens used in client presentations that contributed to new client acquisition
  • Clinicians reported significant time savings in daily patient review workflows
  • Clinicians reported fewer errors when switching between patient data contexts
  • Product audit that mapped the full scope of design inconsistencies across the platform
  • Design system unification research and direction established
