03 — Case Study · AiRXOS, GE Aviation

Designing 4 Surfaces of a Life-Critical Drone Airspace Platform

AiRXOS (part of GE Aviation) builds the airspace management platform used by 200+ US public safety agencies. I designed across web, iPad, and iPhone — as the sole designer, working directly with the CEO.

Overview

The short version

AiRXOS, part of GE Aviation, is an Unmanned Traffic Management (UTM) platform for drone operations. It serves three audiences: drone operators registering flights with the FAA, advanced operators flying beyond visual line of sight, and first responders running emergency drone missions.

I was the only designer on the product. The CEO provided domain expertise — FAA regulations, airspace rules, operational workflows. I translated those use cases into interfaces across 4 surfaces: two web products, an iPad app, and an iPhone app.

Constraints

Worth naming upfront

This case study is honest about what shaped the work.

01

Sole designer

No design team. No design lead. I owned every screen, every interaction, every component decision across 4 surfaces.

02

No user research — use cases instead

I did not conduct user interviews. The CEO had deep FAA and aviation domain expertise and provided use cases that became my design input. That's not how I'd run it today, but it was the reality of the project.

03

Paradox Design System

GE Aviation mandated the use of Paradox, their internal design system. Visual direction was system-constrained, not freeform. The screens look dated today because the system itself was — that wasn't a design choice I owned.

04

Life-critical context

First responders use this in emergencies. Drone operators use it to get FAA authorization. Errors have real consequences. The interface couldn't be ambiguous.

Execution

What I actually did

1. Translated regulatory use cases into screens

The CEO would describe a workflow — "An operator needs to request an airspace authorization, the FAA reviews it, the operator gets approval or rejection, and they need to know when their authorization is about to expire." My job was to turn that into:

  • A registration flow that didn't require pilots to understand FAA jargon
  • A status system that made authorization state glanceable
  • A notification system (the iPhone app) that surfaced expiry warnings before they mattered
AiRXOS web platform — LAANC airspace authorization screen

2. Built the cross-platform information hierarchy

The hardest design decision: what information lives on which surface.

  • Web (LAANC + Beyond Registry): Full operator dashboard. Planning, history, compliance documentation, fleet management. Used at a desk.
  • iPad (First Responder): Mission-critical info. Live airspace map, active flights, mission status. Used in the field, possibly in a command vehicle.
  • iPhone (TFR Notifications): Alerts only. TFR expiry, reroute requests, contract cancellations. Glanceable, push-notification-driven.
First Responder iPad — mission management view with live airspace

3. Designed for the worst-case user state

First responders aren't using this in calm offices. They're at incident scenes, often under stress. I designed for:

  • Glanceable status (color + shape, not just labels)
  • Large touch targets for iPad use with gloves or in motion
  • Persistent critical information (active TFRs always visible)
  • Confirmation patterns for actions that couldn't be undone mid-mission
TFR Notifications iPhone — airspace alerts, expiry warnings, reroute requests
Collaboration

How the CEO collaboration actually worked

This is worth being specific about — it's a real model for working with a domain-expert founder.

The CEO owned

  • FAA regulatory requirements
  • What data a pilot or responder needs in each scenario
  • Operational workflows from real airspace operations
  • Use cases derived from his aviation expertise

I owned

  • Information architecture across 4 surfaces
  • Interaction design and flow
  • Visual design within Paradox constraints
  • Cross-platform consistency
  • Component-level decisions

We didn't have user interviews, but we had a CEO who'd worked in aviation for years. His expertise was the research proxy. That worked because his domain knowledge was real and specific — not opinion-based product preferences.

Outcome

What shipped

  • All 4 surfaces shipped and went live
  • AiRXOS Public Safety UAS Solution rolled out to 200+ US public safety agencies
  • ~70% reduction in flight planning time vs. the previous manual process
  • Product publicly demoed by GE Aviation and the DRONERESPONDERS network

Lessons

What I'd do differently

  • Push for user research, even informally. Even 3–5 conversations with actual public safety drone operators would have changed decisions I made. The CEO's expertise was strong, but it wasn't a replacement for watching real users complete real tasks.
  • Challenge the design system constraint earlier. Paradox limited what I could do visually. I accepted that constraint without questioning whether First Responder — a field app — actually needed to follow GE's enterprise system. A separate, lighter visual layer for high-stress field use might have been a stronger argument to make.
  • Document the system I was building. I designed components for 4 surfaces with no documentation infrastructure. Anyone joining after me would have inherited a pile of Sketch files. The Aampe project later taught me how much that matters — but it would have helped here too.
